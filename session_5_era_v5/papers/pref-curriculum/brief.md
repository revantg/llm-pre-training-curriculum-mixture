## Verdict for our assignment

**This is pretraining, not DPO.** "Preference" here means *the distribution of data that the partially-trained model currently benefits most from* — measured by a perplexity gap between two small reference models. There is no pairwise preference, no reward model, no human labels, no post-training leg of any kind; the whole paper is a next-token-CE pretraining run. It is therefore squarely in scope, and it is the **only paper in our set that isolates pure ordering with zero selection and zero reweighting at 3B/1T scale**: `∫₀¹ αᵢ(p)dp = 1/n` guarantees every token is used exactly once, so any delta is attributable to *sequence alone*.

**Usable numeric value: yes, moderate-to-high, but not where the abstract points.** The headline "+8.1% MMLU+CMMLU, +4.1% average (3B, 1T tokens)" is real but it is almost entirely three highly-correlated Chinese/English knowledge MCQs where the Random baseline never leaves chance (MMLU 27.7, CMMLU 27.5, CEVAL 27.2 vs 25% chance). On ARC-E/ARC-C/SciQ/PIQA the gains are +1.1/+2.1/+0.7/+0.3 and the per-iteration curves (Fig 13a–d) cross repeatedly with visible run-to-run swing of ±1.0 to ±2.5 points, so those four are at noise. One seed, no error bars anywhere in the paper.

**The parts that matter most for our README are the parts the paper treats as side observations.** Its own Fig 6b shows a PD curriculum sends **88% of arXiv and 66% of GitHub into the first half of training and 86% of Wikipedia into the second half** — while §3.5 claims PD "ensures that the data at each training step does not skew towards specific sub-domains." That is a self-contradiction, and it is the cleanest published evidence we have that **a pure difficulty/utility ordering silently re-times whole domains and has no floor mechanism.** It **supports our protected always-on floor**, and it **challenges** the intuition that a "difficulty ladder" moves reasoning and code *late*: on this metric code and STEM are the *easy* half and get spent early. Also usable: the S-shape ablation gives us a principled, numeric answer to "how long should a mixture transition be" (best setting blends over **44% of the run ≈ 880B tokens at 2T**), and a hard switch is measurably *worse* than a blend (Z-shape 53.6 vs S-shape 54.7), which independently corroborates the V4 gradient-spike mitigation.

## Teach this first

**1. "Preference" is the model's, not a human's — and it is a curriculum claim, not an alignment claim.** Everyone pretrains on one fixed data mixture from step 1 to step K. The paper's thesis is that this is wrong because *the model changes*: a 5%-trained model and a 95%-trained model are different learners, so they profit from different data. "Data preference" = which slice of the corpus produces the most useful gradient *right now*. Gloss: this is **curriculum learning (CL)** — order the same data easy→hard — applied to LLM pretraining. *Why it matters for V5:* this is the academic backing for our five-stage curriculum existing at all. It says the win is available from **re-timing tokens we already own**, with no new data and no extra tokens — the cheapest lever in the whole mixture design.
> "as the model's capability improves, it is intuitive that its data preferences dynamically change, indicating the need for pretraining with different data at various training stages."

**2. The metric: Perplexity Difference (PD).** `PD(x) = (PPL_weak(x) − PPL_strong(x)) / PPL_weak(x)` — the *fractional* perplexity reduction a stronger model gets on sample `x`. Gloss: **perplexity (PPL)** = exp(mean token cross-entropy); low PPL = the model finds the text predictable. Low PD means "a bigger model is no better at this text — both models fit it equally" (Fig 10: low-PD text is *also* low-PPL, a sharp mode at PPL≈6 under the 100M model). High PD means "scale helps a lot here." *Why it matters for V5:* PD is a **scale-sensitivity score, not a hardness score.** Anything formulaic — LaTeX preambles, boilerplate source files, templated markup — is low PD because neither model struggles. Anything requiring stored world knowledge is high PD. If we ever put a PD-style selector in front of our corpus, it will rank our Indic and code lanes by *how templated they are*, which is not what we want a difficulty ladder to mean.

**3. The cost trick: two small reference models stand in for two checkpoints of the real run.** Ideally you would score data with the run's own early and final checkpoints, but you do not have the final checkpoint until you are done. The paper argues that what distinguishes an early from a late checkpoint is *trained FLOPs*, and a small model trained on the same data has few FLOPs while a larger one has many — so a **100M model plays "early" and a 700M (or 1.3B) model plays "late"**, both trained on an i.i.d. subset of the same corpus. Fig 4 confirms this substitution empirically: PD from the 100M–700M pair (54.74) actually **beat** PD from the run's own early/end checkpoints (54.69). *Why it matters for V5:* a proxy-model scoring pass is affordable. Our derived cost: with a 100M + 700M pair trained on 100B tokens and one inference pass over 2T, the whole PD pipeline is **≈0.26% of a 120B/2T run's FLOPs** — cheaper than the "few percent" OPUS already costs.

**4. Offline schedule vs live selector — the operational difference from OPUS.** MATES (2406.06046) and JEST (2406.17711) pause training, re-score data against the current model, resume. PDPC refuses to do that: it scores the whole corpus **once, before the run**, fits a closed-form curve `f(p)` over training progress `p = k/K`, and hands the trainer a fully pre-arranged data order. Training never stops and never queries the model. *Why it matters for V5:* PDPC is a **static, pre-planned schedule**, and OPUS is a **live per-iteration selector**. They are not competitors on the same axis — PDPC decides *when* a token is served, OPUS decides *whether* it is served. That is exactly why they compose, and exactly why PDPC provides no answer to the starvation problem OPUS creates.

**5. Do not sort — partition, then blend.** The obvious implementation is "sort the corpus by PD ascending and stream it." The paper tries it (Sequential-PD-Low2High) and it gets **53.1 vs Random's 53.5** — *worse than random*. Reason: a fully sorted stream makes every batch internally homogeneous, and Fig 7b shows top-10%/bottom-10% PD samples occupy visibly separate regions of T5 embedding space. They also note that with duplicates in the corpus, a full sort places identical samples in the *same* batch. So instead: split at the PD median into two buckets, and each step draw `α₁(p)` of the batch from the low-PD bucket and `1−α₁(p)` from the high-PD bucket. Every batch keeps both. *Why it matters for V5:* this is a hard empirical rule for our curriculum stages — **stage boundaries must be mixing ratios, never stream cut-points.** A sorted stream destroys within-batch diversity and gives back the entire gain plus interest.

**6. The S-shape, and what its best setting numerically means.** Three candidate `f(p)` shapes were searched: Linear, Z-shape (hard step at p=0.5), and S-shape `f(p)=1/(1+exp(a(p−0.5)))`. All three beat Random; **S-shape with a=10.0 wins (54.7 vs Random 53.5)**, Z-shape is nearly worthless (53.6), Linear 53.7. Because the sigmoid is symmetric about (0.5, 0.5), `∫f = 0.5` automatically, so the two halves are exactly consumed. Concretely at a=10: the first batch is **99.3% low-PD**, the last is **99.3% high-PD**, and the fraction crosses from 0.9 down to 0.1 over `p ∈ [0.28, 0.72]` — a **43.9%-of-run transition band**. *Why it matters for V5:* this is a real, defensible number for "how gradually must a mixture change." A hard switch (Z) throws away ~1.1 of the 1.2 available points. Our V4 lesson — a sudden Hindi ramp spiked gradient norm ~150× and had to be blended over several billion tokens — gets independent numeric support: the winning curriculum blends over **~880B tokens at 2T scale**, three orders of magnitude longer than "a few billion."

**7. What actually happens to the data, domain by domain — the finding the paper under-reports.** Fig 6b, normalising each *source* across the two PD halves: **ArXiv 88% low-PD / 12% high-PD. GitHub 66/34. StackExchange 55/45. C4 52/48. CommonCrawl 46/54. Book 45/55. Wikipedia 14/86.** Fig 12 shows the per-domain PD means separate cleanly (ArXiv mode ≈0.21, Wikipedia mode ≈0.35, corpus mean ≈0.27). So a "difficulty" curriculum on this metric is, in practice, a schedule that **front-loads code and papers and back-loads encyclopedic knowledge.** Fig 9's GPT-4o property audit agrees and inverts the naive story: high-PD text is *less* likely to require scientific/technical concepts (0.61 vs 0.70) and *less* likely to require cultural background (0.59 vs 0.69) than low-PD text; logical-reasoning requirement is identical (0.72 vs 0.72). *Why it matters for V5:* our curriculum anchors ramp reasoning and long-context **up** in the late stages. A PD-style ordering would do the opposite for code/STEM and would drain those lanes in the first half. If we cite PDPC for "order matters," we must **not** also cite it for "put the hard technical stuff last" — it says the reverse.

**8. Read the result curves before quoting the average.** Fig 3b: PDPC and Random are **indistinguishable for the first ~65,000 of ~190,000 iterations (≈35% of the run)**; the separation is entirely a back-half effect. Fig 5a is even more honest: all three curricula are *worse* than Random in test loss for the first ~40% of the run (Z-shape by up to +0.0033), and the winning S-shape ends only **−0.0018 nats** below Random. Fig 3c/13e: MMLU is pinned at 0.255–0.26 for both arms until ~75k iterations, then PDPC climbs to 0.358 while Random wanders to 0.276. *Why it matters for V5:* a curriculum is a **bet that pays late.** Any mid-run checkpoint eval will look flat or slightly negative. If we adopt an ordering policy for the 2T run, we must state up front that its intermediate evals are not the decision signal — otherwise the first checkpoint review kills it.

## Method, in order

1. **Fix the corpus and the budget.** Take pretraining dataset `𝒟` sampled uniformly (no selection, no filtering beyond whatever the corpus already had). Fix total steps `K` and batch size `N`. PDPC "only arranges the given data without performing data selection… serves as the final data preprocessing step before pretraining."
2. **Train two reference models (RMs) on an i.i.d. subset of `𝒟`.** 1.3B experiment: **100M and 700M** RMs on a **50B-token** i.i.d. subset of SlimPajama. 3B experiment: **100M and 1.3B** RMs on a **500B-token** i.i.d. subset. The smaller RM = the "early checkpoint" role; the larger = the "final checkpoint" role. Justification is FLOPs-equivalence, not architecture identity.
3. **Score every sample twice.** One full inference pass over `𝒟` with each RM to get `PPL_Mw(x)` and `PPL_Ms(x)`; then `PD(x) = (PPL_Mw − PPL_Ms)/PPL_Mw`. Samples with `PPL_Mw < PPL_Ms` (negative PD) are **<0.01% and are ignored** — that is the only data ever dropped.
4. **Sort by PD ascending and split into `n` equal-size parts** obeying `|𝒟ᵢ| = |𝒟|/n` and `∀x∈𝒟ᵢ, ∀y∈𝒟ⱼ, i<j ⇒ PDₓ ≤ PD_y`. The paper uses **n = 2** throughout: `A_PD^low`, `A_PD^high`, split at the **PD median**. `n=1` degenerates to Random; `n=|𝒟|` degenerates to a full sort; `n>2` is left to future work (the grid-search space is `nᵀ`).
5. **Choose the preference function `f(p)`** mapping progress `p = k/K` to the low-PD share, subject to (a) decreasing (CL principle: easy→hard), (b) point-symmetric about (0.5, 0.5) so both parts are exhausted, and (c) `∫₀¹αᵢ(p)dp = 1/n` so **all data is used exactly once**. Candidates searched: `f_L(p)=k(p−0.5)+0.5` with `k∈[−1,0)`; `f_Z(p)= 1−λ` then `λ`, `λ∈[0,0.5)`; `f_S(p)=1/(1+exp(a(p−0.5)))`. Winner: **S-shape, a = 10.0.**
6. **Materialise the whole data order offline.** For every step `k`: `p=k/K`, `[α₁,α₂] ← [f(p), 1−f(p)]`, `B_k = {x∼A_PD^low}_{α₁N} ∪ {x∼A_PD^high}_{α₂N}`. Because this is precomputed, training runs start-to-finish **without interruption** (the explicit contrast with MATES/JEST).
7. **Pretrain.** Llama architecture in Megatron-LM, Adam, batch 640, sequence length 8192, LR 2e-4, cosine decay, warm-up over >375M tokens, weight decay 0.1.
8. **Evaluate** with lm-evaluation-harness, few-shot/in-context following QuRating, plain accuracy: ARC-E, ARC-C, SciQ, HellaSwag, PIQA (1.3B) plus MMLU, CMMLU, CEVAL (3B).
9. **Baselines**: Random (`n=1`); PPL-as-difficulty (two 700M annotators, one per corpus); QuRating Educational Value as difficulty; and Sequential (full sort, ascending or descending, on PD / PPL / Qu.Edu).
10. **Analysis passes** (all post-hoc, all on SlimPajama): source distribution over 25M texts; T5 embeddings + t-SNE on uniform vs extreme-decile PD samples; 4 QuRating raters for quality; GPT-4o yes/no over 10 semantic criteria on 1,000 samples per part; Spearman correlations between PD variants.
11. **(Appendix B, proposed, no results reported.)** An iterative version: build 11 annealing datasets with low-PD share `β ∈ {0%,10%,…,100%}`, each padded with 30% pretrain-distribution samples; anneal 8 checkpoints (listed as `{0, 12.5, 25, 37.5, 50, 62.5, 75, 87.5, 100}%` — nine values, so the count is inconsistent) on all 11; record `b_p = argmax_β M_p(β)`; fit `b=f(p)` with PCHIP (monotone cubic Hermite); rescale the split quantile by `∫f = α` since the fitted integral need not be 0.5; retrain; repeat until `|Δf| < ε`. **No experiment for this is reported anywhere in the paper.**

## Assumptions

- **A fixed, offline schedule is a good enough approximation of the model's live preference.** → Hides that our OPUS selector is live and adaptive, so PDPC's central engineering claim ("no interruption") buys us nothing we don't already pay for. What PDPC actually contributes to V5 is the *shape* of the ramp, not the offline-ness.
- **A weak/strong perplexity gap is a proxy for sample difficulty.** → Hides that it is really a proxy for *knowledge density vs formulaic-ness* (Fig 6, 9, 10). For **agentic trajectories** this is likely to misfire badly: tool-call traces are highly templated (`{"tool": ..., "args": ...}` scaffolding), so both RMs will fit them well ⇒ **low PD ⇒ scheduled early and exhausted early** — the exact opposite of our plan to reserve agentic trajectories for the anneal. The paper never tests structured/agentic data.
- **The corpus is roughly balanced English (or English+Chinese) web-scale text with ≥7 large sources.** → Hides everything about **Indic**. Fig 9 shows low-PD text is *more* culturally dependent (0.69 vs 0.59), which suggests culture-heavy native Indic text would land in the low-PD half and be **front-loaded and then starved**. And with Indic at 12–16% of the mixture, a global median split can push a whole lane almost entirely into one half.
- **Median split at n=2 is sufficient granularity.** → Hides that a two-bucket global split cannot express per-lane schedules at all. Our V5 curriculum needs six lanes each on its own ramp; PDPC's mechanism has exactly one knob (`a`) and one axis (PD).
- **Every token is used exactly once, so total tokens and epoch counts are unchanged.** → Hides the interaction with **repetition**. Our Indic/reason/long-ctx lanes need ~1.2× repeat and agentic needs ~63× repeat-or-synthesise. Repeating a sample changes nothing about its PD, so PDPC will schedule all 63 copies of an agentic sample into the same PD bucket, i.e. into the same half of the run. The `∫αᵢ = 1/n` conservation law is silently broken by any lane that repeats.
- **Two RMs trained on the same data give a stable ranking.** → Hides Fig 11: PD(100M–700M) vs PD(700M–1.3B) Spearman is **0.41**, not "strong." The two pairs sharing the *weak* model correlate 0.95. So the ranking is dominated by the weak model's perplexity, meaning PD is close to "inverse PPL of a small model" — and a small model's PPL on Indic script with a mostly-English tokenizer is a tokenizer artefact, not a difficulty signal.
- **Downstream MCQ accuracy is the right objective.** → Hides that the reported win is concentrated in MMLU/CMMLU/CEVAL, three knowledge benchmarks that the baseline never lifts off chance. Nothing here speaks to code execution (no HumanEval/MBPP), math (no GSM8K/MATH), long context, or agentic success.
- **Single seed, single run per configuration.** → Hides all variance. Fig 13g shows CEVAL swinging 0.247→0.292 across one 5,000-step interval on the *same* run; several reported gaps are smaller than that.

## Implementation nits

- **Architecture / framework:** Llama architecture, Megatron-LM, Adam.
- **Optimiser schedule:** initial LR **2e-4**, **cosine** decay, warm-up over **>375M tokens** (≈72 steps at their batch), weight decay **0.1**. No LR value at the end of cosine, no β₁/β₂, no grad-clip value, no z-loss — despite gradient norm being a headline analysis. **No separate anneal/cooldown leg is described anywhere in the main experiments**; the annealing that appears in Appendix B is a *probing* tool (11 short anneals used to read off the model's preferred ratio), not a final high-quality cooldown. So this paper gives us nothing directly on the anneal reserve.
- **Batch / sequence:** batch **640**, context window **8192**. ⇒ 5,242,880 tokens/step, 190,735 steps for 1T (matches Fig 3b). The 1.3B figures run to 25,000 steps = 131B tokens at that batch, against a stated 100B — unreconciled.
- **Packing:** never described. Nothing about document concatenation, EOS handling, cross-document attention masking, or whether an 8192 window is filled with multiple samples. This matters a lot for PDPC because PD is computed **per sample** (`PPL` averaged over the sample's `L_x` tokens) while training consumes **fixed 8192-token windows**; if two samples from opposite PD buckets are packed into one sequence, the batch ratio `α` is only approximate. Unaddressed.
- **Length bias:** `PPL` is length-normalised but `PD` is a ratio of two length-normalised quantities, so short samples will have higher variance in PD. No length control, no minimum-token filter, no report of the length distributions of the two halves. For our long-context lane (32k–128k documents) this is untested territory: nothing in the paper scores a document longer than the 8192 window.
- **Reference models:** 1.3B experiment → 100M + 700M RMs on a 50B-token i.i.d. subset of SlimPajama. 3B experiment → 100M + 1.3B RMs on a 500B-token i.i.d. subset. RM architecture, tokenizer, and hyperparameters are not stated separately; presumably the same Llama/Megatron recipe. The "Early-End" ablation used early and late checkpoints of a *randomly*-trained 1.3B.
- **Data:** SlimPajama (627B deduped RedPajama) for 1.3B; a 1T bilingual mix (500B zh + 500B en) drawn from Pile books, Pushshift Reddit, BigPatent, FineWeb CommonCrawl, Wikipedia, "similar to the Matrix dataset" (MAP-Neo, 2405.19327) for 3B. **The Chinese/English balance is 50/50 and no per-source percentages are given for the 1T mix** — so we cannot reuse their mixture weights, only their ordering result.
- **Evaluation:** lm-evaluation-harness, few-shot in-context following QuRating, plain accuracy. Shot counts not stated. HellaSwag is dropped from the 3B table without comment (it is the one benchmark PDPC regressed on at 1.3B: 33.7 vs 34.2).
- **Analysis tooling:** T5 for embeddings + t-SNE; 4 QuRating raters; GPT-4o for the 10 semantic criteria (prompt reproduced in Appendix C.5); PCHIP (`scipy.interpolate.pchip`) for the Appendix-B fit.
- **Code / artifacts:** **none.** No GitHub link, no released PD scores, no released data order, no model weights. Everything here has to be reimplemented from the equations — which is easy (Eq 2 + Eq 8 + a median split) but means no independent verification exists.

## Follow-on papers

- **2406.06046 — MATES: Model-Aware Data Selection with Data Influence Models.** The live-selector this paper is arguing against; it interrupts training to re-score against the current model. Would change a V5 number by telling us whether re-scoring *during* the 2T run beats PDPC's frozen schedule — i.e. whether OPUS should also carry a difficulty/ordering signal instead of just a keep/reject signal.
- **2406.17711 — JEST: Data Curation via Joint Example Selection.** Selects *batches* jointly rather than samples independently, and reports large effective-token multipliers. Directly relevant to OPUS's ~6× effective-token claim and to whether our per-batch composition (not just per-sample keep rate) should be optimised.
- **2402.09739 — QuRating.** Supplies the four quality raters used in Fig 8 and the Educational-Value baseline that scores +0.4 against PD's +1.2. Would change a V5 number by settling whether our lane-internal ordering should use a quality rater (cheap, already have it) or a two-model PD gap (needs an extra scoring pass).

## Assignment mapping

**1. Budget share for every capability lane (code, agentic, reasoning, long-context, Indic, STEM, general web = 100%).**
**Silent, and mildly cautionary.** The paper deliberately does not touch mixture weights: *"PDPC only arranges the given data without performing data selection"* and `∫₀¹αᵢ(p)dp = 1/n` fixes every part's total share. It reports **no** per-source percentages for its 1T mix beyond 500B zh / 500B en. Do not cite it for any lane share. Do cite it for the claim that **lane shares and lane timing are separable decisions** — its whole +4.1 comes from timing at constant shares.

**2. Indic split across verified / unverified / translated / synthetic.**
**Silent.** No multilingual analysis, no low-resource language, no script-level anything. The nearest thing is a 50/50 Chinese/English corpus with no per-tier breakdown, and Fig 9's finding that **low-PD text is more culturally dependent (0.69 vs 0.59)** — which is a reason to *worry* that a PD-like signal would systematically front-load culture-heavy native Indic text, not a basis for tier percentages. Also note Fig 11's 0.41: PD's ranking is dominated by the weak (100M) model's perplexity, and a 100M model's perplexity on Devanagari with a mostly-English tokenizer measures tokenizer fertility, not difficulty. **Any PD-style score must be computed per-lane, never globally, or the Indic tiers get sorted by tokenizer artefacts.**

**3. Agentic, reasoning, long-context as first-class pretrain slots.**
**Silent on the slots, and it challenges our timing assumption.** The paper has no agentic data, no CoT, no long documents (context is 8192 and PD is computed per sample). It *does* support the general principle that pretraining data can be scheduled by capability-relevant properties. But the concrete evidence cuts against a naive "hard stuff last" reading: logical-reasoning requirement is **identical** in both halves (0.72 vs 0.72, Fig 9), so PD cannot even find our reasoning lane; and highly-templated data — which agentic trajectories are — will score low-PD and be scheduled **early**. Our plan to reserve long CoT and agent trajectories for the anneal is **not** supported by this paper and must be justified from Two-Phase/OpenCoder instead.

**4. Protected always-on floor the OPUS selector cannot starve (Indic + agentic + reasoning).**
**Strongly supports — and this is the paper's biggest contribution to our README.** PDPC has **no floor of any kind**, and its own Fig 6b shows what that costs: *"ArXiv 0.88 low-PD / 0.12 high-PD … Wikipedia 0.14 / 0.86 … GitHub 0.66 / 0.34."* Two-thirds of the code lane and 88% of the papers lane are consumed in the first half of training; 86% of the encyclopedic lane waits for the second half. Meanwhile the paper asserts the opposite: *"Partitioning and organizing data using PD ensures that the data at each training step does not skew towards specific sub-domains, allowing the model to encounter a diverse range of data throughout the entire training process."* **That sentence is contradicted by the paper's own Figure 6b and Figure 12** (per-domain PD modes ArXiv 0.21 vs Wikipedia 0.35 around a 0.27 mean). This is a published, unforced demonstration that a single global utility/difficulty score silently re-times entire domains while its authors believe it does not. That is the argument for our floor: **a floor is required precisely because the selector's designers cannot see the skew they are creating.** Our floor should therefore be enforced *per lane per batch* (V4's Indic-at-8%-of-every-batch design), extended to agentic and reasoning, and — new from this paper — the floor should be **two-sided**: a lane needs a minimum share not only overall but *in every stage*, or PD-like dynamics will front-load it and leave the late run empty of it.

**5. Anneal reserve: which best data is held back for the cooldown.**
**Silent, verging on unhelpful — say so plainly.** There is no cooldown, no LR-decay leg on curated data, no held-back reserve. The word "annealing" appears only in Appendix B, where 11 short anneals over `β ∈ {0%,…,100%}` low-PD share are used as a **measuring instrument** to read off `b_p = argmax_β M_p(β)` at 8 checkpoints, then PCHIP-fitted — and **no results for this procedure are reported at all**. Two things are still transferable: (a) the design detail that every probe dataset was *"supplemented with 30% of samples that share the same distribution as the pretraining data"* — i.e. even a deliberately extreme anneal mix kept 30% of the base distribution, which is a reasonable prior for our anneal reserve not going 100% curated; and (b) the mechanism itself is a cheap, legitimate way to *choose* our anneal mixture empirically — run short anneals at several candidate mixtures off one checkpoint and pick the argmax. Cite it as a method, never as a number.

**6. Each lane tied to the benchmarks it is meant to win.**
**Partially supports, with a sharp caveat.** It gives a real, causal chain from an ordering decision to specific benchmarks: high-PD data is Wikipedia/CommonCrawl-enriched (Fig 6a: Wikipedia 0.01→0.05, CC 0.56→0.65), and the benchmarks that move are exactly the knowledge MCQs — MMLU +8.1, CMMLU +8.1, CEVAL +8.9 — while ARC-E/ARC-C/SciQ/PIQA move +1.1/+2.1/+0.7/+0.3 and are visibly inside noise (Fig 13a–d). The paper says as much: *"The significant performance improvement of PDPC in later stages is largely attributed to the higher proportion of high-quality data from sources like Wikipedia, which primarily appears in high-PD data."* Usable lane→benchmark rule: **encyclopedic/knowledge tokens late buy MMLU-family accuracy; commonsense and science-QA benchmarks are insensitive to ordering.** Caveat for the reviewer: an "average across 7 benchmarks" that is really 3 correlated knowledge MCQs is not a capability average. **Do not import PDPC's ±4.1 average into any V5 lane justification; import the per-benchmark numbers.**

**7. Demand vs unique token supply; where a share only works by repeating or synthesising.**
**Silent, and structurally blind to it.** PDPC assumes single-epoch consumption of a fixed corpus: `∫₀¹αᵢ(p)dp = 1/n` means each part is used exactly once and **0% of data is discarded** (only <0.01% negative-PD samples ignored). It never models repetition, and it warns about the one place repetition surfaces: *"if the dataset contains duplicate samples, a complete sort would likely place identical samples in the same batch, which is detrimental to improving pretraining efficiency."* That is directly load-bearing for our agentic lane — 40B demand against 0.63B unique tokens is ~63× repetition, and repeated copies share an identical PD, so a PD-ordered schedule would drop all 63 copies into the same bucket and therefore the same half of the run, clustering near-duplicates. **Explicit V5 rule to write down: repeated tokens must be spread across the schedule by an epoch-aware shuffle applied *after* any difficulty ordering, and near-duplicate clustering must be checked at the batch level.** The paper flags the hazard and does not solve it.

## What not to overclaim

- **Do not say "ordering alone buys +4.1 average / +8.1 MMLU" without the baseline context.** The Random baseline is at chance on all three benchmarks that generate that number (MMLU 27.7, CMMLU 27.5, CEVAL 27.2 vs 25% chance), the four remaining 3B benchmarks move +1.1/+2.1/+0.7/+0.3 with per-interval eval noise of ±1.0–2.5 points (Fig 13a–d, 13g), the 1.3B run's gain is only +1.2 and includes a HellaSwag *regression* (−0.5), and there is **one seed and zero error bars in the entire paper**. The defensible claim is: *at 3B/1T, reordering a fixed corpus easy→hard moved knowledge-MCQ accuracy from chance to ~36% and left commonsense/science QA unchanged.*
- **Do not call PD a difficulty or quality metric, and do not present PDPC as a difficulty ladder that puts reasoning and code last.** By the paper's own measurements, high-PD text requires *less* scientific/technical content (0.61 vs 0.70) and *less* cultural background (0.59 vs 0.69) than low-PD text, logical-reasoning requirement is identical (0.72/0.72), QuRating quality distributions are nearly indistinguishable, and 88% of arXiv plus 66% of GitHub fall in the "easy" half. PD measures **how much stored knowledge scale buys you on this text** — it front-loads code and papers and back-loads encyclopedias. Also do not call PD "stable": the Spearman between the 100M–700M and 700M–1.3B variants is **0.41**.
- **Do not treat this as evidence about the anneal, long context, agentic data, or Indic, and do not quote a compute overhead as if the paper gave one.** There is no cooldown leg, context is capped at 8192, PD is per-sample with no length control, packing is never described, no code is released, the corpus is English or English+Chinese, and the paper reports **no** overhead figure — the ~39% (their 3B setting) and ~0.26% (a 120B/2T run with a 100M+700M pair) numbers above are my `6ND`/`2ND` estimates, marked `[INFERENCE]`, not the authors'. Finally, the "no interruption" selling point is irrelevant to us: OPUS is already a live per-iteration selector, so PDPC's contribution to V5 is the ramp *shape* (S-shape, a=10, 44%-of-run blend band, hard switch −1.1) and the starvation warning in Fig 6b — nothing else.
